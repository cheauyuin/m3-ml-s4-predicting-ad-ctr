Predicting Ad Click-Through Rate

Will a user click this ad — yes or no? A binary classification problem, and a case study in why the obvious metric (accuracy) is the wrong one.

40.4M ad impressions Avazu CTR dataset · 10 days Solution design focus
The Problem

Every ad shown is a bet

Every time an app or website shows you an ad, that's called an impression. The advertiser only makes money if you click it.

If we can predict how likely a given impression is to be clicked before showing it, we can pick better ads to show, price ad slots fairly, and stop wasting impressions on people who were never going to click.

That's the whole task: given what we know about an impression (site, device, time of day...), predict the probability of a click.

In one line

Input: an ad impression's context.
Output: probability of click, 0-100%.

Why it's hard

Clicks are rare — most impressions are ignored. That single fact shapes every design decision that follows.

The Trap

Why "accuracy" lies to you

Across our data, only ~17% of impressions get clicked. The rest — 83% — don't.

The trap

A model that always predicts "no click" is right 83% of the time — 83% "accurate" — yet it never identifies a single click.

Our stand: we evaluate with AUC and log-loss — never plain accuracy.

Click rate varies by hour — a real pattern (not noise, at this sample size), but a modest one: 16.0%-18.6%, oscillating around the 17.0% average.

Reading The Chart Carefully

Is this really about "time of day"?

Each dot is one hour. If time of day were the whole story, this cloud would show no slope; instead it leans down-right — hours with more concentrated site-category mix tend to have lower click rates (r = -0.53, p = 0.008). Real, but loose: the trend line explains only ~28% of the variance (r²), so most of the hourly pattern is still genuinely about time, not just inventory mix.

Three deliberate design choices sit behind this pipeline — the next slides walk through each, and why we made that call.

Design Decision · 1 of 3

We split by time, not at random

The data spans 10 days. A random 80/20 split mixes future and past together — the model can quietly "peek" at patterns from days it should never have seen yet.

Day 29
Validate
Day 30
Test

Our stand

Train on the past, tune on the next day, and judge final performance on a day touched exactly once, at the very end. This mirrors how the model would actually be used — predicting tomorrow from today — and it's a much harder, more honest test than a random shuffle.

Design Decision · 2 of 3

Two kinds of features, two treatments

Low-cardinality context

Site category, device type, banner position, hour of day — a handful of possible values each. Simple to encode directly (one-hot).

7 features

High-cardinality IDs

Site ID, device ID, device model — thousands of distinct values. Too many to one-hot encode, but their raw ID is meaningless anyway.

5 features, frequency-encoded

Our stand — leak-safe by construction

We encode each ID as "how often has this been seen?" — a useful signal without exposing the click label. Critically, that frequency count is learned only from the training days, then applied unchanged to validation/test. No peeking, no leakage.

Design Decision · 3 of 3

Two models, on purpose

Logistic Regression

A simple, linear, highly interpretable model. Fast, transparent, and — as we'll see — extremely stable. Kept as the trustworthy baseline.

LightGBM

A gradient-boosted tree ensemble. Captures interactions a linear model can't (e.g. "mobile + evening + this site category"). Tuned with regularization and stopped early based on validation performance — not a guessed tree count.

This pairing lets us show the class a real trade-off: simplicity & stability vs. capacity & performance — not just pick "the best model" and move on.

Behind the Numbers

What actually drives clicks

This chart shows how much each piece of information matters to LightGBM's predictions — longer bar, bigger influence.

  • Device model, site ID, and device IP (all frequency-encoded) top the list — which specific site or device matters more than any single category.
  • Hour of day also ranks highly — consistent with the click-rate-by-hour pattern from earlier.
  • The low-cardinality context fields (device type, banner position, anonymized category) matter least on their own — their value comes from combining with everything else, which is exactly what a tree-based model like LightGBM is built to do.
Is it trustworthy?

Checking for overfitting, explicitly

A model can look great on training data and fall apart on new data. We report the gap on purpose, instead of hiding it.

Diligence Checks

We tested the "obvious upgrade" — target encoding

Instead of just proposing target encoding as future work, we built it (5-fold cross-fitted, leak-safe) and ran it head-to-head against frequency encoding, same split, same model.

EncodingTrain AUCTest AUCTest log-lossTrain-Test gap
Frequency (adopted)0.7550.7280.4090.027
Target (tested)0.7770.7300.4060.047

Our stand

Target encoding wins on both test metrics — but only marginally (+0.002 AUC), while the overfitting gap nearly doubles and it costs ~9× longer to compute (5-fold cross-fitting per column vs. an instant frequency count). Given our goal was a stable model, we shipped frequency encoding and kept target encoding as a documented, deliberately-rejected alternative — not a shortcut we skipped.

Calibration — checked

Model over-predicts at every level (Brier 0.127) — fine for ranking, but would need recalibration (Platt/isotonic) before pricing off the raw probability.

What's Next

Where we'd take this further

Stronger smoothing

Tune target encoding's smoothing prior further — might keep most of the AUC/log-loss gain while narrowing the overfit gap.
